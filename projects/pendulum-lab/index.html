Pendulum Lab: a self-driving pendulum experiment

Yagyaansh Khaneja
B.Tech Engineering Physics, Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee

September 2026

A camera watches a pendulum that hangs from the arm of a hobby servo. With no human in the loop, the rig runs its own experiments to identify the pendulum's physics, choosing each experiment by Bayesian D-optimal design; estimates the state from video that arrives about 150 ms late; and uses the identified model to control the swing amplitude. On the physical rig the controller met its target in 19 of 19 trials, modelling the servo and the latency cut the amplitude error by 95%, and the camera's timing agreed with an independent light gate within 0.1%. The pre-registered 2× speed-up from self-chosen experiments was not reached (about 1.5×), and the damping law could not be identified. The rig was built in four days from parts already owned.

1. What it is

A camera watches a pendulum that hangs from the arm of a hobby servo. With no human in the loop, the system:

  1. identifies the pendulum's physics from its own experiments, choosing each next experiment by Bayesian D-optimal design, under a safety constraint;
  2. estimates the pendulum's state from video frames that arrive about 150 ms late, using an extended Kalman filter with delayed measurements and forward prediction;
  3. controls the swing amplitude with an energy-shaping controller built on the identified model.

The rig is an Arduino UNO, an SG90 micro-servo, a 30 cm steel ruler as the pendulum, a breadboard power module, a photoresistor light gate, and a Sony α7 IV used as a USB webcam. The hypotheses and pass/fail criteria were written down before any hardware data existed, and every failure is reported against them.

2. Results on the physical rig

QuestionResult
Can the identified model control the swing?Yes: 19/19 trials at 15°, 25° and 35° reach and stay within ±2° in 4–12 s (95% CI 82–100%, requirement ≥ 80%).
Is the camera's timing trustworthy?Yes: the swing period from the camera matches an independent light gate, timed by the microcontroller's clock, within 0.07–0.09%.
Does the identified model agree with an independent measurement?Yes: the identified model gives a small-swing period of 0.934 s; the light gate measured 0.933 s.
Does modelling the servo and the latency improve control? (H2)Yes: 95% lower amplitude error (median 0.57° against 12.0°, 7 interleaved pairs, p = 0.016). Without the model the controller never reached a target (0/7).
Do self-chosen experiments identify the system twice as fast as a standard sweep? (H1)Not as pre-registered. D-optimal design reached the held-out noise floor in a median of 10 s against 15 s for a chirp: faster in 5 of 6 pairs, about 1.5× rather than 2× (p = 0.06).
Can the damping law be identified? (H3)No: viscous and quadratic damping are indistinguishable on held-out data (16 runs, p = 0.43).
Camera latency ≤ 150 ms (go/no-go)Failed as measured: 151–164 ms median. Control works regardless.
Static angle accuracy ≤ 1°Failed as measured: 1.42° RMS against a hand-held ruler aligned by eye.

The criteria were frozen before the hardware data. Three were revised once, after the simulation campaign and before any hardware identification; the revision is labelled in the requirements, and every failure above is reported against the criterion as written.

3. What was built and debugged

  • The whole stack: firmware, perception, the delayed-measurement estimator, grey-box identification, Bayesian experiment design, control, the statistics, and a 3D build guide. 89 automated tests.
  • A servo that browned out the board when powered from it.
  • A camera that overheated and silently re-zoomed after restarts; fixed with a 15 s calibration check before every session.
  • A "holding" servo that kept pumping energy into the pendulum; fixed with a firmware command that makes it go limp.
  • Frame-timestamp jitter that made the controller chatter; fixed by making the estimator's trust depend on speed.
  • A bias in the experiment design itself (the smarter method got free data while it was "thinking"), caught and removed before the comparison was run.

The main lesson: on hardware the actuator matters more than the model of the pendulum. The remaining gap between simulation and reality comes from the servo's dead band and compliance, not from the physics of the swing.

4. Next steps

  • an encoder as the angle reference;
  • a lower-latency camera;
  • modelling actuator compliance;
  • perturbation experiments in which the rig notices a change (added mass, a shorter rod) and re-identifies itself.
