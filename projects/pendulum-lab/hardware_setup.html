Hardware Setup and Replication Guide

This guide explains how to build the pendulum rig from scratch.

  • 3D companion: rig_setup.html. Open it in a browser to orbit the rig, animate it, and see the planned webcam framing.
  • Nominal dimensions: ../configs/sim.yaml. Replace them with your measured values (§9) once the rig is built.

Principle: the rig must be rigid everywhere except the pivot. The pivot must turn freely, with almost no play. Everything else is validated against independent references (tests E0–E1 in test_plan.md) before any experiment is trusted.

2. The servo: what it is and how it is commanded

An SG90 is a position-controlled motor. Inside it are:

  • a DC motor;
  • a plastic gearbox;
  • a potentiometer that measures the output angle;
  • a small controller that drives the motor until the measured angle equals the commanded angle.

The simulator models this internal loop as a second-order system with a rate limit (≈ 60° per 0.1 s) and a dead time (see src/pal/sim/dynamics.py).

Commanding it: send a pulse on the signal wire 50 times per second. The pulse width sets the angle:

Pulse width Angle (approximate)
1000 µs −45°
1500 µs centre
2000 µs +45°

Wires:

Wire Meaning Connect to
Brown Ground (−) Ground rail, shared with the UNO
Red +4.8–6 V External 5 V rail (§6)
Orange Signal UNO D9

Never twist the servo shaft by hand, because it strips the plastic gears. Keep commands within 1000–2000 µs while testing. Pushing a servo against its end stop makes it buzz and heat up.

3. Mechanical build

3.1 Bare servo test and centring (nothing attached)

  1. Install Arduino IDE 2. Plug in the UNO and select Tools → Board → Arduino Uno and its Port. If no port appears, install the CH340 USB driver.
  2. Wire the bare servo directly to the UNO: brown → GND, red → 5V, orange → D9. Male-male jumpers push into the servo's connector.
    • This is the only time the servo may be powered from the UNO. An unloaded SG90 draws little current, and ELEGOO's own servo lesson does the same. Once anything is attached, use external power (§6).
  3. Upload ../firmware/servo_test/servo_test.ino.
  4. Open Serial Monitor at 115200 baud. Type 1000, 2000, then 1500: the shaft should swing about ±45° and return to centre. Leave it at 1500.

3.2 Horn, pointing straight down

  1. With the servo holding 1500 µs, push the single-arm horn onto the shaft, pointing straight down.
  2. The shaft is toothed, so pick the tooth position closest to vertical. A few degrees of offset is corrected in software.
  3. Fix the horn with the tiny screw into the shaft.

3.3 Arm and servo mounting

  1. Fix the arm to the horn with screws through the horn holes, plus glue if needed. Any sliding or hinged part of the arm must be locked.
  2. Screw the servo by its two ear tabs to the front face of a wooden block, with the shaft pointing at the camera.
  3. Clamp or weigh the block down at the table edge, so the arm and pendulum hang just in front of the edge.
  4. Cover or remove any sharp points on the arm.

3.4 Pivot axle: the critical joint

The axle is fixed in the arm, and the rod turns on the axle.

Side view (backdrop on the left, camera on the right)

 backdrop              arm (fixed to the servo horn)
    │                   │   washer
    │                   │   │   rod (hole 0.2-0.5 mm larger than the axle)
    │                   │   │   │   axle head / nut + GREEN marker
    │   axle tip ───────█───▪───█───●   ───►  towards the camera
    │  (taped over)     │       │
    │                   │       │   rod hangs down and swings left-right,
    │   ~3 cm gap       │       │   in the plane facing the camera
  1. Position: the axle goes directly below the servo shaft when the arm points down, at the arm length (nominally 7 cm).
  2. Direction: horizontal, square to the arm, pointing straight at the camera. A tilted axle tilts the swing plane.
  3. Fit in the rod: choose an axle 0.2–0.5 mm narrower than the rod's hole. Measure the hole first.
    • The reason: the servo accelerates the pivot sideways at up to ~2–3 g (estimate), so a loose hole makes the rod rattle.
    • The model assumes a rigid pivot. It can identify friction, but not looseness.
  4. Axial room: about 1 mm of sliding room between the washer and the head. More lets the rod wobble toward the camera; less makes it rub.
  5. Tip: tape over the axle tip behind the arm, and check that it cannot touch the backdrop.
  6. Marker: put the green marker on the axle head, so the camera sees the true pivot position (servo gear play included).

3.5 Which joints may move

Joint Allowed motion
Servo horn ↔ arm None: screwed and glued
Arm ↔ axle None: fixed tight, no wiggle or spin
Axle ↔ rod Free rotation, almost no play
Block ↔ table None: clamped or weighted
Servo's internal gears Some play is unavoidable. It is measured through the green marker.

3.6 Rod, bob and markers

  1. Hang the rod on the axle.
  2. Magenta marker: put it near the bottom of the rod. With a 30 cm ruler, that is about 25–28 cm from the pivot. Record the exact distance (§9).
  3. Optional bob: tape 1–3 nuts near the bottom.
  4. Light-gate vane: tape a 5 × 5 cm cardboard vane at the bottom, so it passes through the light-gate beam at the lowest point of the swing.
  5. Optional second pivot hole: drill one 5 cm lower, for the "shortened pendulum" perturbation test (E6).

3.7 Backdrop

  • Tape the matte black card to the table edge, hanging behind the pendulum, about 3–4 cm behind the swing plane. The rod must never touch it.
  • Run every wire along the backdrop edges, outside the swing area. Wires behind the pendulum end up in the middle of the camera frame.

4. Protractor (calibration test E1a only)

  1. Print print/protractor_R250_A4x2.pdf at actual size on A4 (not "fit to page"). If regeneration is needed, run python scripts/make_protractor.py.
  2. Check scaling: on each page, the horizontal and vertical check bars must be equal in length.
    • Exactly 100 mm is ideal but not required, because angles survive uniform scaling. (A4 printed on a printer set to Letter comes out at about 95% and is still fine.)
    • Unequal bars mean the print is distorted. For example, 95% × 100% turns the 45° line into about 43.5°. Reprint on A4.
  3. Assemble:
    1. Tape page 1 (LEFT) down, ideally on a window.
    2. Cut page 2 (RIGHT) along its dashed line.
    3. Lay page 2 over page 1 so the thick 0° line, the top crosshair and the bottom cross align. Holding the pages against daylight makes this easy.
    4. Tape the seam. The arc must continue across the seam without a step.
  4. Mount: put the crosshair directly behind the pivot axle, and rotate the sheet until the 0° line runs along the rod hanging at rest.

The protractor covers ±45° at a 250 mm radius. 1 mm of alignment error is ≈ 0.23°, well inside the 1.0° accuracy requirement (R-S3).

5. Light gate and latency LED

  • Light gate:

    • The white LED sits on the backdrop at vane height, shining towards the camera.
    • The photoresistor sits on a stand (books or cardboard) about 12 cm in front, facing the LED.
    • The vane cuts the beam at the bottom of the swing.
    • Photoresistors respond in tens of ms, so the gate is used for the period (test E1b), where constant delays cancel.

  • Latency LED: the red LED goes in the top-right corner of the backdrop, inside the camera frame. The board toggles it and the host measures when the camera sees it (E1c).

  • Shield the photoresistor. It is very sensitive: in a dim room, the red LED's glow 5–7 cm away changed the reading visibly. So:

    • keep the photoresistor close to and facing its white LED;
    • put a short paper or black tube (about 1–2 cm) around it as a blinker;
    • keep the latency LED and the lamp out of its line of sight.

  • Measured response (2026-09-27, photoresistor on the + side). Median A0 over 8 s each:

    Light A0
    Room light 657
    Phone torch 941
    Fingertip covering 141

    Covered vs lit differ by about 800 counts; the firmware threshold goes between them, with hysteresis.

  • As built (2026-09-28). A simpler layout than the one above also works:

    • the white LED is taped at the bottom edge of the protractor sheet, about 28 cm below the pivot, near the ruler's tip;
    • the photoresistor is 1–3 cm in front of it, its face pointing at the LED, with the ruler's path in the gap;
    • no vane: the ruler itself (about 2.5 cm wide) blocks the beam.

    Tape the LED down. An LED hanging by its legs sways, and the reading changes with it.

  • Gate test with a real swing (2026-09-28). Recorded by polling A0 at about 244 Hz over serial:

    Condition A0
    Beam clear (rod held aside) 851 ± 2
    Ruler resting in the beam ≈ 245 (drifts 160–250 with room light and people moving)
    Fast pass, released from about 30° Dips only to 540–580, lasting 16–25 ms
    Slow pass, near the end of the swing Dips to 300–400, lasting 50–240 ms
    • The photoresistor is too slow to settle during a fast pass, so the thresholds must sit just below the clear level: 720 blocked / 790 clear in configs/rig.yaml.
    • The midpoint thresholds (300/450, or the 2/3 point that C calibration sets) catch almost nothing.
    • All 29 passes were detected at 720/790. The full period from every other passage was 0.933 ± 0.004 s, slightly longer at larger amplitude, as expected.
    • If a future setup gives shallower dips, add the 5 × 5 cm vane: a wider blocker gives the photoresistor more time.

6. Electrical wiring

The servo gets its own 5 V supply. The UNO is powered by USB (logic and data only). Only the grounds are joined.

Why this matters (measured on 2026-09-26/27). With the servo on the UNO's 5V pin, every servo move pulled the UNO's supply from 5.14 V down to 4.41 V, even for 1° steps and even with nothing attached. The cause is the motor's start-up current spike. With the servo on the separate module below, the dip fell to 0.02 V (5.09 V → 5.07 V), about 30× smaller.

6.1 Servo power: kit breadboard power module (used here)

The ELEGOO breadboard power module is a small black board with:

  • a barrel socket ("Vin < 12V"), a USB-A socket, a grey push-button and a green LED;
  • two jumper blocks marked "5V VCC 3.3";
  • a 2 × 4 pin block in the middle. One row is marked GND. The other row sits beside the "5V" and "3.3V" labels, with a bracket under each pair.

The middle block's outputs are fixed: they do not depend on the jumper caps, and no breadboard is needed.

From To Jumper type
Kit 9 V 1 A adapter Module barrel socket —
Servo red (+) Module middle block, a pin under the "5V" bracket (not 3.3V) Female end onto the pin (the servo extension ribbon's female end fits directly)
Servo brown (−) Module middle block, GND row Female end onto the pin
UNO GND Module middle block, GND row (another pin) Male-to-female
Servo orange (signal) UNO ~9 Male-to-male (from the ribbon's female end)
UNO 5V Nothing —
UNO USB Laptop —

Power-up order:

  1. Plug the adapter into the module and press the grey button. The green LED lights.
  2. Then plug in the UNO's USB.

Check before the first power-up: red is under 5V, and brown and the UNO's ground are on the GND row. Reversed polarity destroys the servo instantly.

Regulator heat: after a few minutes of servo motion, touch the module's two small 3-legged black chips. Warm is fine; too hot to hold means stop.

6.2 Sensors and indicator LEDs (breadboard, hole by hole)

Signal Circuit
Photoresistor UNO 5V → photoresistor → A0 → 10 kΩ → UNO GND
Gate LED (white) UNO D7 → 220 Ω → LED long leg; short leg → GND
Latency LED (red) UNO D8 → 220 Ω → LED long leg; short leg → GND

These run from the UNO's own 5V, which is clean because the servo is not on it. Do not put the servo power module on this breadboard: it would join the servo's supply to the sensors' supply.

Breadboard orientation:

  • Holes are named by column letter (a–j) and row number (1–63).
  • Holes a–e of one row are connected to each other.
  • Beside column a there are two long columns of holes, and each is connected along its whole length. Between them and the board's edge run a red line and a blue line.
    • The + strip is the hole column directly beside the red line, usually the outer one.
    • The − strip is the hole column directly beside the blue line, next to column a.
    • Check the red/blue lines on your board rather than assuming the order.
  • Electrical check (recommended): with io_test running, temporarily plug the breadboard end of the A0 jumper into the strip you believe is +. It must read a steady 1023; on the − strip it must read about 0. This caught a wrongly powered strip during bring-up.

UNO pins used:

  • 5V and the free GND on the power header (the other GND carries the ground wire to the power module);
  • A0 on the analog header;
  • D7 and D8 on the digital header.

Steps (USB and servo power off):

  1. Male-to-male jumper: UNO 5V → + strip (near row 5).
  2. Male-to-male jumper: UNO GND → − strip (near row 5).
  3. Photoresistor: one leg into the + strip (the column beside the red line) near row 10, the other into a10. The photoresistor itself has no polarity.
  4. 10 kΩ (brown–black–orange): b10 → − strip (the column beside the blue line) near row 12. The photoresistor and the 10 kΩ must end up on opposite strips. If both are on −, A0 reads about 0 whatever the light (this happened during bring-up).
  5. Jumper: c10 → UNO A0.
  6. Jumper: UNO D7 → a20.
  7. 220 Ω (red–red–brown): b20 → b24.
  8. White LED: long leg c24, short leg − strip near row 24.
  9. Jumper: UNO D8 → a30.
  10. 220 Ω: b30 → b34.
  11. Red LED: long leg c34, short leg − strip near row 34.

Count check: 5 jumpers, 3 resistors, 2 LEDs, 1 photoresistor.

Common questions and checks:

  • Any hole in the same row works. a10–e10 are all the same connection, so an LED leg can go in a24 instead of c24 if its legs are short. What matters is that the LED's long leg shares a row with its resistor's far end, and not with the D7/D8 jumper's row (that would bypass the resistor).
  • A reversed LED simply stays dark. With the resistor in series it is not damaged. Flip it.
  • Photoresistor and 10 kΩ swapped between the + and − strips (still on opposite strips): this works, with an inverted reading (bright gives a low value). Both on the same strip does not work.
  • The 10 kΩ, the photoresistor and the A0 jumper must share one row (row 10 here). If the resistor sits one row off, A0 reads a constant value.
  • The + strip must come from the UNO's "5V" pin, not RESET or IOREF, which are two and three holes above it.

Test: upload ../firmware/io_test/io_test.ino.

  • The white LED should be on, the red LED should blink at 1 Hz, and the A0 reading is printed at 115200 baud.
  • Covering the photoresistor must change the reading clearly.

Final placement: extend the LEDs and the photoresistor to their positions (§5) with male-to-female jumpers. The female end goes onto the component's leg; the male end goes into the same breadboard hole the leg used.

6.3 Alternative servo supply: phone charger

If no power module is available:

  1. Cut a spare USB cable from a 5 V ≥ 2 A phone charger. Strip the red (+5 V) and black (GND) wires, and insulate the others.
  2. Check polarity first by putting an LED with a 220 Ω resistor across the wires. It lights only when its long leg is on +5 V.
  3. Use a breadboard: charger red/black → + / − rails; servo red/brown → + / − rails; UNO GND → − rail.
  4. Put a 100 µF capacitor across the rails near the servo, with its − leg on the − rail.

Never connect the UNO's 5V pin to the servo's supply, and never power the loaded servo from the UNO. Only the grounds are joined.

7. Camera and lighting

  1. Placement: the laptop screen faces the rig, about 75 cm from the pendulum plane. Raise the laptop on a stool or books so the webcam lens is about 22 cm below the servo shaft.
  2. Framing: the servo sits near the top of the image, and the full ±60° swing fits inside the 640×480 frame. At this distance the frame covers about 80 × 60 cm, which is 8 px/cm. Use Webcam's view in rig_setup.html to compare.
  3. Camera settings: manual exposure, 4–8 ms (OpenCV exposure −8 to −7 on the tested webcam). Longer exposures blur the markers: at ≈ 1 m/s, 33 ms smears them ≈ 3 cm.
  4. Lamp: a bright DC LED lamp above and to the front-left, at about 45°.
    • Avoid glare on the steel rod and the markers.
    • Avoid mains-powered flickering lamps: 50 Hz mains in India flickers at 100 Hz.
    • Lock the exposure; auto-exposure changes the frame rate.

7.1 What must be in the frame

In the frame? Item Why
Must Servo shaft and the green dot on the pivot, over the full arm travel Calibration fits a circle to the green dot to find the shaft, the pixel scale and the servo zero
Must The pink dot over the whole swing, at least ±60°, plus about 3 cm of margin A dot leaving the frame means lost measurements, exactly at the large angles that matter most
Must The red latency LED Test E1c measures the camera delay from it
Optional The white gate LED and the photoresistor The board reads the gate; the camera does not need it. It may be in view (a white LED is not mistaken for a marker), but its glow must not wash over the pink dot
  • Size, with the arm at 7.2 cm and the pink dot at 25 cm. The pink dot reaches about ±27 cm sideways (21.7 cm from the swing, plus 5 cm from the arm at ±45°).
    • It reaches 32 cm below the shaft.
    • So the frame should cover about 75 cm wide × 42 cm tall (16:9), with the servo shaft about 5 cm below the top edge, centred left–right.
    • The two protractor pages together are about 42 cm wide, so they fill a little over half the frame width.
  • Keep the camera square to the swing plane. The lens axis should be perpendicular to the backdrop, at about the height of the middle of the swing. Tilt makes angles read wrong.

7.2 As built: Sony α7 IV (ILCE-7M4) over USB (2026-09-28)

The α7 IV streams as a standard USB webcam ("USB Video Device"); OpenCV opens it directly (DirectShow index 2 on this laptop, next to the built-in webcam and the OBS virtual camera). Measured: 1280×720 MJPG at 30.0 fps; requests for 1080p or 60 fps were ignored, because the camera sets the stream format itself.

Camera settings. Menu names are from Sony's α7 IV help guide; check them on the body.

  1. Stream format: MENU → Setup → USB → USB Streaming → Output Resol/Frame Rate → HD (1080p) 60p, if offered. 60 fps doubles the samples per swing; tell the host to re-probe after changing it.
  2. Exposure: manual (M) in movie mode, shutter 1/250 s (4 ms). This matches camera.exposure: -8 (2⁻⁸ s ≈ 3.9 ms) in configs/rig.yaml, which the fit uses for mid-exposure timing. Set brightness with ISO, not the shutter.
  3. Focus: manual (MF), focused on the rod.
  4. SteadyShot: off. The camera is fixed, and sensor-shift stabilisation would move the image and make the markers jitter.
  5. White balance: a fixed preset, not auto, so the marker colours stay put.
  6. Power: streaming drains the battery. Use a charged battery, or USB-C power delivery if the laptop port supplies it. Raise the auto power-off temperature if it overheats.
  7. Lighting: with a 4 ms shutter, 50 Hz mains lamps flicker at 100 Hz, and it shows as brightness changes between frames. Prefer a DC LED lamp (see step 4 above).

Session routine (lessons from 2026-09-28):

  • Stabilisation off on a tripod: lens OSS switch OFF and SteadyShot OFF. With it on, each power-up re-centred the image by 10–30 px. Switching it off also changed the zoom by about 12%.
  • After every camera restart or touch, first check: experiments/check_calibration.py (about 15 s). It holds the arm vertical, waits for rest, and compares the live feed with the saved calibration: green marker position (≤ 3 px), rod length in pixels (≤ 1%, zoom), resting angle (≤ 0.3°, roll) and arm angle at 0 and ±20° (≤ 1.5°, the SG90's repeatability).
  • Only if it prints FAIL, recalibrate: experiments/calibrate_rig.py --rest-wait 35 --write.
    • Check the printed rod/arm in the image line: it should be within about 8% of the metric ratio (about 2.9). If it says CHECK, a wrong object was taken for a marker.
    • Check that the servo trim is close to its previous value (about −71 µs). A big change means the horn or arm moved.
  • Then a quick latency check: experiments/e1c_latency.py --toggles 30. Update camera.capture_latency if it moved by more than about 5 ms.
  • Heat and power: the α7 IV switched itself off twice in about 20 minutes of streaming.
    • Let it cool between long sessions.
    • Use a full battery or external USB-C power.
    • Set Auto Power OFF Temp. to High and the power-save start time to maximum.
    • The control loop stops safely if frames stop (0.5 s), but the remaining trials are lost.

First frame check (2026-09-28). Camera on a tripod with a telephoto zoom, about 1.5 m in front of the swing plane (measured later: §9): the long lens keeps perspective distortion small. Results:

  • Framing: good. The frame covers about 85 × 48 cm, at 15 px/cm. The green dot, the pink dot's ±60° swing and the red LED are all inside.
  • Both markers were detected wrong, because each colour matched something bigger:
    • the "green" match was the green tape roll on the desk behind (HSV 37/162/119), not the green dot (30/200/64, dark);
    • the "magenta" match was a pink or purple patch below the sheet, behind the ruler's tip, not the pink dot.
    • The pink dot measured hue 4, which is red, not magenta (140–172). That is the same hue as the red LED, the red wires and the bricks.
  • The white sheet read orange (hue 16, saturation 76), so the white balance was far off and all colours were shifted warm.

Fixes, in order:

  1. Remove anything green, pink or purple from the frame (the tape roll, the patch below the sheet).
  2. Light the rig with the LED panel from about 45° to the side. Directly beside the lens, it glints off the steel ruler.
  3. Custom white balance on the white protractor sheet; the sheet should then read neutral. If that is not possible, set the panel to daylight (≈ 5600 K) and match that preset on the camera.
  4. Re-check the dot colours. If the pink still reads red (hue below 10 or above 175), re-colour it with a purple-pink or magenta pen, or retune the markers: HSV boxes in configs/rig.yaml to the measured colours with experiments/e0_camera.py.

After the fixes (same day). Custom white balance on the sheet and a higher ISO; the sheet now reads V ≈ 225.

  • Measured colours:

    • pink sketch-pen dot: hue 0–4, S 80–110;

    • both markers found in 150/150 frames;
    • centroid jitter 0.10–0.14 px RMS;
    • θ noise 0.024° RMS, against a 1° requirement;
    • tracking time 1.6 ms per frame (the first frame, a full-frame search, takes 51 ms).

8. Acceptance checks (do these in order)

# Check Pass criterion
1 Bare servo test (§3.1) Smooth motion to 1000/2000/1500 µs, no buzzing at rest
2 Wiggle test With the arm held still, the rod turns freely but does not click or rattle sideways on the axle
3 Free swing (rig mounted, not hand-held) Released from about 30°, it swings for over 20 s, staying in one plane, with no wobble toward or away from the camera
4 Loaded servo test Commands of 1450 / 1550 / 1500 µs with the rod hanging: no UNO reset, no twitching, no constant buzzing, servo not hot
5 Shake test (once the firmware can oscillate the servo) No clicking; the green marker does not jump in the video
6 Framing Servo shaft, green dot, the pink dot at ±60° (plus margin) and the red LED are all inside the frame (§7.1)

Then run the validation experiments E0–E1 from test_plan.md.

Troubleshooting:

Symptom Likely cause and fix
UNO resets, or the supply dips, when the servo moves The servo is powered from the UNO, or the ground is not shared. Fix the wiring per §6.1.
Swing dies within a few seconds The axle is too tight, or the rod rubs the washer or head. Adjust the clearances in §3.4.
Swing wobbles out of plane The axle is not square to the arm, or there is too much axial room.
Clicking during shaking The rod hole is too loose for the axle. Use a thicker axle.
Blurry markers Exposure is too long. Add light and shorten the exposure.

10. Safety

  • Keep your face and hands out of the swing zone (about 50 cm around the pivot) while the servo is powered.
  • Unplug the charger before rewiring, and check polarity before connecting the servo.
  • Cover sharp points on the arm and the axle tip.
  • If the servo gets hot or buzzes continuously, cut power and look for binding or overload.
